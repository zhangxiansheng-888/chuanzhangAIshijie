# Performance camera language

Use camera choices to reveal motivation and emotional progression. Tie every camera change to a dramatic beat.

## Motivation model

Define four causes before writing facial movement:

1. Trigger: the event the character has just perceived
2. Goal: what the character wants from the other person
3. Obstacle: why the character cannot respond directly
4. Protective strategy: hide, joke, appease, attack, withdraw, freeze, or collapse

Example:

`Trigger: she hears a cruel accusation. Goal: preserve dignity. Obstacle: she still cares about the speaker. Protective strategy: suppress the hurt and push the person away.`

This creates a readable conflict between restrained eyes, compressed lips, and a delayed verbal rejection.

## Emotional progression

Use this sequence for short dialogue shots:

1. Baseline: establish the face before the trigger registers
2. Recognition: gaze or blink shows that the character understood
3. Resistance: the character tries to preserve the current social mask
4. Leakage: one channel betrays the hidden emotion
5. Apex: the line, decision, or loss of control
6. Residue: show what remains after the line

Avoid changing all channels together. Lead with gaze and eyelids, then mouth and jaw, followed by breath, head, shoulders, or hands.

## Shot size

| Shot | Best use | Expression guidance |
|---|---|---|
| Extreme close-up | tiny recognition, eye moisture, lip tremor | keep movement minimal and avoid large head motion |
| Close-up | most emotional dialogue | balance eyes, mouth, jaw, and a small amount of shoulder movement |
| Medium close-up | defensive posture, retreat, hand near chest | increase facial clarity slightly because the face occupies less of the frame |
| Profile close-up | avoidance, secrecy, emotional distance | use eye movement and jaw tension that remain visible from the side |

## Camera behavior by emotional function

### Static hold

Use for numbness, disbelief, suppressed grief, or confrontation. The lack of camera reaction forces attention onto microexpressions.

### Slow push-in

Use when recognition deepens, a social mask begins to fail, or the character makes an internal decision. Begin after the trigger registers and stop before the facial apex.

### Slow pull-back

Use when disappointment creates emotional distance, the character withdraws, or a relationship feels irreparable.

### Slight handheld drift

Use for vulnerability, uncertainty, panic, or emotional breakdown. Keep the movement small enough that eyelids and lips remain readable.

### Brief reactive jolt

Use for a sudden verbal blow, shock, or an explosive line. Apply it once and let the camera settle quickly.

### Reframing and negative space

Use growing negative space to express separation, isolation, or emotional absence. Let the character drift toward one side of the frame only when the story motivates withdrawal.

### Focus behavior

Keep focus on the nearer eye for most close-ups. A short focus hesitation can support shock or dissociation. Avoid repeated focus hunting that hides subtle performance.

## Angle and relationship

1. Eye level supports intimacy, ambiguity, and emotional equality
2. A slight high angle can increase vulnerability or exposure
3. A slight low angle can reinforce controlled threat or defiance
4. A frontal close-up supports confrontation
5. A three-quarter view supports conflicted attention and avoidance
6. A profile supports withdrawal and emotional separation

Keep angle changes subtle unless the scene deliberately shifts power.

## Eyeline and screen direction

State where the other person or object sits in screen space:

1. Near-lens eyeline creates intimacy or confrontation
2. A target on frame right places gaze toward the right edge and preserves matching negative space
3. A target on frame left does the reverse
4. A lowered target can imply shame, submission, a seated person, or an object in hand
5. A distant target reduces eye convergence and can support memory, dissociation, or emotional withdrawal

Keep eyeline direction stable unless the character's attention deliberately changes.

## Lighting as performance support

Translate emotional tone into physical light:

1. Warm soft side light supports intimacy, relief, and concealed affection
2. Cooler background light with a neutral face supports disappointment and separation
3. Harder side light and deeper jaw shadow reinforce controlled anger
4. Moving candlelight, passing shadow, or unstable practical light can support fear or breakdown
5. Low-contrast side light and broad shadow support numbness without hiding the eyes
6. A gradual shadow crossing the face can mark realization when motivated by a moving curtain, flame, cloud, or person

State key light direction, softness, color temperature, and shadow behavior. Avoid unexplained color changes or studio-perfect beauty lighting.

## Six emotional camera recipes

### Playfully scolding

Use an eye-level close-up with a gentle, almost imperceptible push-in as the suppressed smile appears. Keep the frame intimate and stable.

### Aggrieved and hurt

Use a close-up with a slow push-in beginning when the breath catches. Hold focus on the eyes and stop moving before the spoken line.

### Disappointed

Use a three-quarter close-up with a very slow pull-back as the gaze lowers and the body retreats. Let slightly more negative space appear.

### Angry

Use a frontal close-up at eye level or a very slight low angle. Add a restrained push-in as the jaw sets, then lock the frame during the line.

### Breaking down

Use a close-up with slight handheld drift. Allow one small reactive recoil at the vocal break, then remain close enough to read the uneven mouth and chin tremor.

### Emotionally numb

Use a locked-off close-up or profile close-up. Avoid pushing in. Hold the shot after the line and preserve empty space in the direction of the absent gaze.

## Failure checks

1. Do not add movement only because it sounds cinematic
2. Do not orbit around a speaking face during a subtle expression
3. Do not use strong handheld shake for restrained emotion
4. Do not change shot size at the same instant as every facial action
5. Do not let shallow focus blur both eyes during the emotional apex
6. Do not use a dramatic low angle for vulnerability without story support
7. Do not let camera motion continue after it has served its emotional beat
